## 1. Precedents for conditional-commitment launches of member organizations

### Group Health Cooperative of Puget Sound (1945-48)
Source: HistoryLink Part 2, https://www.historylink.org/File/7546 [fetched]; Part 1, https://www.historylink.org/File/7531 [fetched]; https://historylink.org/File/2912 [fetched].
- Incorporated as a nonprofit membership corporation 22 Dec 1945, "not a true cooperative" with equity shares [fetched, File/7531].
- Early 1946: "memberships cost $75 each, sold as lifetime memberships to a few hundred 'cooperators.'" A director and two salesmen were hired to sell memberships [fetched, 7546].
- Sept 4, 1946 membership meeting approved buying the Medical Security Clinic (hospital, clinics, staff, "more than 10,000 contract enrollees") for $190,000 [fetched, 7546].
- Fee structure after acquisition: "$100 membership fee, plus $3 per month dues for each adult family member and $1.50 for each of the first four children" [fetched, 7546].
- Recruitment target: 800 members in a year. Actual: "At the end of its first year of operation, Group Health listed 8,500 group plan enrollees and 1,024 cooperative members" [fetched, 2912]. 1948: membership 2,811 [fetched, 7546].
- Financing: sold $100 "Pioneer Bonds" to raise $100,000 to pay off the clinic purchase; sellers cut the balance by $80,000 and took one-fourth of the rest in bonds; debt cleared Nov 1948 [fetched, 7546].
- Takeaway: founding capital came from (a) lifetime memberships sold before service existed, (b) a one-time $100 membership fee plus monthly dues, and (c) member bonds. The acquisition of an existing clinic with 10,000 enrollees is what made day-one service possible.

### New credit union charters (NCUA)
Source: https://ncua.gov/support-services/credit-union-resources-expansion/chartering [fetched]; 12 U.S.C. 1753 via https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/12/1753 [snippet]; https://ncua.gov/news/publication-search/annual-report/2024-annual-report [fetched]; https://ncua.gov/newsroom/press-release/2025/ncua-charters-haven-federal-credit-union [snippet].
- Statute requires "seven or more natural persons" (subscribers) to sign a sworn organization certificate [snippet].
- Phase 1 (review goal 60 days): define field of membership, describe capital funding plans, two name choices; "Federally chartered credit unions must have an NCUA-approved field of membership" [fetched].
- Phase 2: "identify subscribers, survey potential members and analyze results, analyze market conditions, secure donated capital, obtain evidence of critical sponsor commitments" (sample commitment letter provided), then business plan on NCUA Form 4001 with pro formas [fetched]. The page does not use the phrase "pledged deposits"; the member survey plus sponsor commitment letters are the conditional-commitment analog [fetched].
- Phase 3: review goal 180 days from complete Phase 2 to charter [fetched].
- Volume: "The NCUA granted four new federal charters in 2024" [fetched]. 2025: Haven FCU (Santa Clara, effective Dec 8, 2025) "is the third federal credit union chartered in 2025" [snippet, NCUA release]. 2023: three [snippet, Tyfone].

### Electric cooperatives under the REA ($5 membership)
Source: https://www.cooperative.com/remagazine/articles/Pages/Then-Now-Getting-a-Missouri-Co-op-Off-the-Ground-Month-by-Month.aspx [fetched]; local co-op histories [snippet].
- Platte-Clay Electric (MO): incorporated Aug 27, 1938 by 22 leaders; field men recruited members; "Membership cost $5, no small sum in the height of the Depression"; Sept 28, 1938: 273 + 100 sign-ups; Nov 10: 663 certificates accepted; Jan 1939: "With 1,367 membership certificates in hand, the board voted to borrow $487,000 from the Rural Electrification Administration"; lights on late fall 1939 [fetched]. Roughly 5 months from incorporation to loan vote, 15 months to service.
- Craighead Electric (AR): REA required 612 members at $5 each before the corporation could apply for a loan; some paid in chickens and eggs [snippet, https://craigheadelectric.coop/?p=1049].
- Gate City (TX): $5 membership plus minimum $2.45/month bill [snippet, https://www.spec.coop/history-gate-city-electric-cooperative-inc — page 404'd on fetch].
- Pattern: a fixed small membership fee collected up front from a threshold number of households, used to unlock outside (federal) capital.

### Health Republic Insurance of New Jersey (ACA CO-OP)
Source: https://whyy.org/articles/new-insurance-options-headed-to-new-jersey/ [fetched]; https://nj.gov/dobi/pressreleases/pr160912.html [fetched]; CMS 2012 release and Empire Center [snippet].
- Feb 22, 2012: federal government "putting up $107 million in low- and no-interest loans"; Freelancers Union's "total loan award was for $340 million" across NJ, NY, OR [fetched]. Entity was "Freelancers CO-OP of New Jersey" d/b/a Health Republic [snippet, NJBIZ].
- Sept 12, 2016: DOBI sought rehabilitation citing "hazardous financial condition, arising in part from a liability of $46.3 million under the Affordable Care Act's Risk Adjustment program"; 26,000 individual and 9,000 small-employer members; "the carrier will not offer coverage for 2017" [fetched]. Liquidation order Feb 3, 2017 [snippet]. Lesson: large federal capital, no member capital; risk-adjustment exposure killed it.
- Comparable: Common Ground Healthcare Cooperative (WI) received $56.4M ($48.9M solvency, $7.4M startup under 96 milestones); first-year goal 10,000 enrollees [fetched, https://biztimes.com/cooperative-on-pace-for-obamacare/?amp=1]; reportedly signed 24,000+ in first OE [snippet, wisconsinhealthnews].

### Ithaca Health Alliance / Ithaca Health Fund
Source: https://www.geo.coop/node/153 (Jan 2, 2008, by IHA staff/board) [fetched]; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ithaca_Health_Alliance [fetched, citation-needed tagged]; https://projects.propublica.org/nonprofits/organizations/900192978 [fetched].

### Assurance contracts / dominant assurance contracts
- Tabarrok, "The Private Provision of Public Goods via Dominant Assurance Contracts," Public Choice 96 (1998) 345-362: entrepreneur produces if enough pledge; if not, refunds plus a "refund bonus," making pledging "a no-lose proposition" [snippet].
- Cason, Tabarrok, Zubrickas lab experiments: refund bonuses to early contributors "can increase the rate of campaign success by 50% or more" [snippet, Purdue WP 1326 / Bath]. No real-world DAC deployment found; LessWrong commenter: "I don't know of any real-world instance of dominant assurance contracts being used" [snippet].
- Kickstarter is a plain assurance contract. Groupon's "tipping point" is another: deal only honored if minimum buyers reached, otherwise "the consumer is not charged" (Groupon patent language) [snippet]; a 2009 Boston lobster deal failed at 7 of 35 needed [snippet, Gazette].

## 2. Crowdfunding mechanics and law

### Kickstarter
- All-or-nothing: "No one will be charged for a pledge towards a project unless it reaches its funding goal" [fetched, https://help.kickstarter.com/hc/en-us/articles/115005028514]; "No one is charged until a project meets its funding goal and the funding period ends" [fetched, https://help.kickstarter.com/en-us/articles/16236590-what-does-kickstarter-do-to-protect-its-community].
- Fees: official page https://www.kickstarter.com/help/fees returned HTTP 403. Third-party: 5% platform fee on successful projects; processing "3% + $0.20 per pledge OR 5% + $0.08 per pledge under $10"; 60-day max duration [fetched, https://merchantmaverick.com/reviews/kickstarter-review]; "5% platform fee ... Applied to funds raised on successful campaigns" [fetched, PledgeBox May 10, 2026]. Human should confirm on kickstarter.com/help/fees.
- Rules: https://www.kickstarter.com/rules and /rules/prohibited both 403'd. A reproduction of the May 12, 2026 rules [fetched, https://graphicpolicy.com/2026/05/14/kickstarter-attacks-adult-comics-while-updating-its-rules/]: "Projects must create something new to share with others"; "A portion, up to 10%, of campaign funds may be donated to charity provided this is disclosed"; prohibited: "Equity"; "Items claiming to diagnose, cure, treat, or prevent a serious illness or condition"; and "Service Providers: Projects cannot be centered around establishing and providing services." The older rule said "Investment is not permitted on Kickstarter." Merchant Maverick list adds "offering financial, phone, travel, and business marketing services" as prohibited [fetched].
- Implication: a membership for ongoing primary-care services almost certainly fails the "service providers" rule and would need to be framed as creating something (unlikely to pass). Kickstarter looks unsuitable; a self-run or specialist platform is the realistic route.
- Pledge Over Time (2025): pledges >= $125 USD split into three monthly payments, first charged when the project funds [snippet, Kickstarter help].
- 2025 year in review gives no platform-wide stats [fetched, https://updates.kickstarter.com/a-year-in-review-2025-kickstarter-highlights/]. Historic success rate ~39-41% [snippet].

### Indiegogo
- Content Policy bars alcohol/tobacco, political campaigns, illegal products/services, pyramid schemes, and content prohibited by payment partners; does not name health/insurance [snippet, https://www.indiegogo.com/en/terms/contentpolicy — page body did not render on fetch]. Third-party lists add "Health Supplements or Medication," "Investments" [snippet]. Terms version seen is Dec 20, 2021 [snippet]. Fixed vs flexible funding and 2026 fees: not confirmed.

### Open Collective
- "Setting up a Collective is free, but fees may apply to accept and pay out money"; host fees commonly 0-10%; "15% of Host Fees charged by Fiscal Hosts goes to the platform"; voluntary Platform Tips [fetched, https://docs.opencollective.com/help/about/pricing]. Processor fees separate [snippet].
- Open Collective Foundation (the US 501(c)(3) fiscal host) dissolved effective 12/31/2024; last donations Mar 15, 2024; funds had to move or be spent by Sept 30, 2024 [snippet, community reposts]. Open Source Collective and OC Europe continue [snippet]. No "pledge held uncharged" feature confirmed; OC is a transparent-ledger tool, not an escrow.

### Stripe authorize-but-not-capture
Source: https://docs.stripe.com/payments/place-a-hold-on-a-payment-method [fetched]; https://docs.stripe.com/payments/extended-authorization [fetched].
- Standard online card hold: Visa CIT 7 days / MIT 5 days; Mastercard, Amex, Discover 7 days. ACH does not support auth/capture. Expired auths cancel and release funds [fetched].
- Extended authorization: up to 30 days; "offered to users on IC+ pricing"; Visa all-other-categories carries +0.08% and "doesn't apply to transactions ... related to healthcare, bill payment, or debt repayment"; networks say extended windows are "only for cases where you don't know the final amount" [fetched].
- Conclusion: card holds cannot span a months-long pledge window. Practical designs: (a) save a payment method (Setup Intent) and charge only if/when the goal is met — a "pledge" is then a saved card plus a signed commitment, not a hold; (b) collect into a true escrow/trust account with refund-on-failure. NOT VERIFIED which escrow vendors serve this niche; the search found none. Academic note: escrow with mandatory ex-post verification is one of two dominant platform designs in a Management Science study [snippet, https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/epdf/10.1287/mnsc.2019.3482].

## 3. New Jersey law touchpoints (all for counsel)

### Charitable Registration and Investigation Act, N.J.S.A. 45:17A-18 et seq.
Source: FindLaw copies, current to Jan 1, 2024 [fetched]: https://codes.findlaw.com/nj/title-45-professions-and-occupations/nj-st-sect-45-17a-20.html ; -23 ; -26. Official DCA PDF https://www.njconsumeraffairs.gov/Statutes/Charitable-Registration-and-Investigation-Act.pdf fetched as binary, unreadable here.
- Timing (45:17A-23b): "It shall be unlawful for any charitable organization to solicit contributions or have contributions solicited in its behalf ... before the Attorney General has been given the opportunity to review the registration statement." Renewal "annually within six months after the close of the charitable organization's fiscal year" [fetched].
- "Charitable organization" includes 501(c)(3)s and anyone who "holds himself out to be, established for any benevolent, philanthropic, humane, ... social welfare, public health, or other eleemosynary purpose" [fetched, -20]. A community health org pitched as public-health/mutual-aid likely fits even without 501(c)(3).
- "Contribution" excludes "bona fide fees, dues or assessments paid by members provided that membership is not conferred solely as consideration ... for making a contribution in response to a solicitation" [fetched, -20]. Key design point: if pledges are genuine membership dues for services, they may fall outside "contribution"; if "membership" is just a label for a donation tier, they are contributions. Pledges count: "contribution" means "the conveyance, promise or pledge of money" [fetched].
- $10,000 exemption (-26c): no registration if gross contributions do not exceed $10,000 in a fiscal year AND "all of its functions, including fund raising activities, are carried on by volunteers, members" or uncompensated officers; on exceeding $10,000, must register "within 30 days after the date on which it shall have received the contributions" [fetched]. DCA page restates this [snippet, https://njconsumeraffairs.gov/charities/Pages/charities-registration-information.aspx — page body did not render].
- Religious and accredited educational institutions exempt [fetched, -26a,b]. Audit required above $500,000 gross revenue [snippet].

### Insurance / discount medical plan / prepaid health plan characterization
- NJ health insurance definition N.J.S.A. 17B:17-4; HMO Act N.J.S.A. 26:2J; DPC Frontier notes 17:48H-1 (organized delivery systems) defines "financial risk" so that capitation payments "shall NOT be considered financial risk," which "appears to exempt DPC but has not been tested in court" [fetched, https://www.dpcfrontier.com/new-jersey, undated ~2019]. NOT VERIFIED current.
- Discount medical plan organizations: I found no enacted NJ DMPO statute. The phrase appears only in unenacted "Health Care Provider Network Transparency Act" bills (A372 2010, A235 2012, A433 2018), which define a DMPO as an entity charging "fees, dues, or other consideration" for discounted provider access [snippet, legiscan/njleg]. The brief's "P.L. 2007, c. 227" cite could not be matched; counsel should pull it. Many other states (RI 27-74, NH 415-I, SC 37-17, MO) register DMPOs [snippet]. DOBI 2014 consumer release warns of discount-card scams with red flags like "The advertised monthly fee is extremely low" but says nothing about licensing of discount programs [fetched, https://www.nj.gov/dobi/pressreleases/pr141022.html].
- Prepaid health services for a fixed fee is the classic "is it insurance" question. Ithaca's NYSID episode (section 1) and DOBI's 2020 action against Aliera/Trinity Healthshare [snippet, https://nj.gov/dobi/pressreleases/pr201223.html] show regulators act on fee-for-coverage arrangements. Risk shifting (paying for care members need) vs. fixed-scope service subscription (DPC) is the line counsel must draw.

### Direct primary care status in NJ
- No DPC "not insurance" statute. DPC Frontier: "New Jersey technically has no DPC laws at present. None are planned in the current legislative session" [fetched, undated]. Extended searches for 2024-26 NJ DPC bills found none [snippet]. Other states (MN HF2880 2025, TX HB541 2025, NE LB817, ME LD1511, WI AB-8/SB-4 2025) have such bills/laws [snippet].
- NJ precedent in practice: the state's own SHBP/SEHBP members get a DPC benefit through Marathon Health with "Zero membership fee and no copays," including an office in West Orange, set up by Plan Design Committee resolutions (SHBP July 6, 2015; SEHBP Jan 11, 2016), not statute [fetched, https://www.nj.gov/treasury/pensions/dpcmh.shtml; snippet for resolutions]. This is useful: DPC already operates for West Orange school employees under the state plan.
- Other NJ physician-practice rules flagged by DPC Frontier: 7-day dispensing limit (45:9-22.11), lab billing (45:9-42.41a), Medicare opt-out notice (45:9-22.15), malpractice minimums $1M/$3M (45:9-19.17), corporate practice of medicine enforced (Allstate v. Northfield, NJ 2017) [fetched]. A lay member-owned entity employing physicians must be structured around CPOM.

### Entity options
- No general cooperative corporation statute found. DMLP (2013): NJ "limits the formation of cooperative corporations to cooperative housing corporations" (N.J.S.A. 46:8D-1) and suggests LLC, corporation, or nonprofit corporation with cooperative features [fetched, https://www.dmlp.org/print/15867]. The 1881 workers' cooperative law, R.S. 34:17, exists; S120 (2024) would modernize it (seven or more NJ residents; adds "service" businesses; removes $50 par cap) and is stuck in committee [fetched, https://pub.njleg.state.nj.us/Bills/2024/S0500/120_I1.HTM]. Most NJ co-ops incorporate under the Business Corporation Act (14A) or Nonprofit Corporation Act (15A) [snippet]. Group Health itself was a nonprofit membership corporation, not an equity co-op [fetched].
- Federal tax: 501(m) — a (c)(3) or (c)(4) is exempt "only if no substantial part of its activities consists of providing commercial-type insurance"; carve-outs for insurance "substantially below cost to a class of charitable recipients" and "incidental health insurance provided by a health maintenance organization" [snippet of statute via Faegre/Tax Notes]. LTR 202015035 (2020) denied (c)(3) to a would-be health insurer [snippet]. A 2022 ruling denied (c)(3) to a health-sharing org priced by market research [snippet]. Ithaca Health Alliance is a (c)(3) [fetched]; Freelancers Union is a (c)(4) "civic league or social welfare organization" [snippet, ProPublica EIN 20-3491772]. Subchapter T (IRC 1381-1388) lets a taxable corporation "operating on a cooperative basis" deduct patronage dividends; it is not tax exemption [snippet]. No on-point authority found for a DPC-membership co-op; NOT VERIFIED which fits best.

## 6. Fraud/trust design

- Kickstarter changes: Sept 2012 "Kickstarter Is Not a Store" added a mandatory "Risks and Challenges" section and barred hardware renderings/multiple-quantity rewards [snippet, NPR]. June 2014 simplified to three rules (create something to share; honest and clearly presented; no charity fundraising, financial incentives, or prohibited items) and added algorithmic "Launch Now" [snippet, TechCrunch/Hypebot]. Trust & Safety today: "monitors the system for suspicious activity," tracks "fulfillment track record," can revoke "the ability to run another campaign on Kickstarter," displays notices on project pages, and "No one is charged until a project meets its funding goal and the funding period ends" [fetched, help article 16236590]. Suspension cancels all pledges; backers not charged [snippet, help article 115005136354]. 2014 iFind suspension ($546,852 from 9,771 backers) for self-pledging/misrepresentation [snippet].
- FTC v. Chevalier (D. Or. 3:15-cv-01029, June 2015): goal $35,000, "raised more than $122,000 from 1,246 backers"; 14 months of progress updates then cancellation with no refunds; funds spent on rent, moving, other projects; order bars misrepresenting "any crowdfunding campaign" and failing to honor refund policies; $111,793.71 judgment suspended [fetched, FTC press release]. FTC's Jessica Rich: "consumers should be able to trust their money will actually be spent on the project they funded" [fetched]. Later: iBackPack case (~$800,000) [snippet].
- FTC consumer alert (May 2019): research the creator's background and previous campaigns; report to FTC and state AG [fetched, https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2019/05/avoid-crowdfunding-scams]. No FTC business-guidance page on crowdfunding disclosures found.
- Disclosure checklist implied by the above (inference, NOT VERIFIED as any rule): who the organizers are and their track record; exactly what a pledge buys (membership for defined services vs. donation); the goal(s), deadline, and what "fail" means; that nothing is charged until the goal is met and how (saved card vs. escrow); refund policy and who holds funds before launch; a "risks and challenges" section (regulatory status, physician recruitment, lease); that it is not health insurance and does not replace coverage (NJ DOBI and FTC both stress this for discount plans); CRI Act registration status; use-of-funds and reporting commitments; identity verification of organizers.
