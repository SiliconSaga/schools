# Medical travel, purchasing options, and a pledge launch

## 1. What organized medical travel has actually looked like

**The Cancún model is real, small, and of unknown current status.** [fetched, KFF Health News Aug 12, 2019; IMTJ 2017 and 2019] North American Specialty Hospital leased a floor of the JCI-accredited Galenia hospital in Cancún, flew in US orthopedic surgeons (about 40 signed up; one was paid $2,700 per knee, about three times Medicare), bought extra US malpractice cover so patients could sue in the US, delivered pre- and post-operative care in the US, and sold bundled surgery at roughly half US prices (knee about $12,000 against about $30,000; implant $3,500 against about $8,000; hospital night $300 against about $2,000). Ashley Furniture's plan waived all cost sharing, paid the employee $5,000, and covered travel; it had sent 10 people to Cancún and about 140 to a Costa Rica hospital since 2016, claiming $3.2 million saved. NASH itself had treated "a couple of dozen" Americans in three years. Its website is live in 2026 with an Omaha address but nothing dated after March 2023; whether it still operates is NOT VERIFIED.

**Costa Rica** has the larger employer volume (Ashley's 140) and two JCI hospitals, Clínica Bíblica and CIMA, but the only prices found are broker listings (knee roughly $15,000 to $27,000, inclusions unstated) [fetched broker page, unreliable].

**Cayman Islands.** Health City Cayman Islands (Narayana Health) published all-in prices in 2016 to 2017: bypass $31,680, hip or knee $15,840, readmissions absorbed, JCI accredited [fetched, 2016 to 2017 sources]. It ran at about 20% capacity, its US partner Ascension exited in 2017, and its 2026 site lists a new hospital but no prices and no US employer deals. The most interesting Caribbean option on paper, with no current evidence.

**Puerto Rico** is the overlooked one: a US jurisdiction, no passport, Medicare-certified hospitals, mostly US-trained physicians, and a 2014 government study claiming costs 40% to 60% below the mainland [fetched, IMTJ 2014]. A 2026 Puerto Rico law reportedly eases licensure for physicians licensed in a US state [snippet, text not read]. No employer program found. For an ERISA plan it is domestic, which removes most of the legal and optical problems of going abroad (inference; confirm with counsel).

**Canada is not a surgery destination.** Private clinics may treat foreigners (clinic-side claim) at roughly C$20,000 to C$28,000 for a joint [fetched, CBC via repost 2023], which is no cheaper than Oklahoma's posted cash price, and the system is capacity-constrained: 65% of hip and knee replacements done within the 26-week benchmark nationally in 2025 [fetched, CIHI]. Ontario's cancer centres take international patients case by case with prepayment and explicit priority to residents, with no price list [fetched, UHN]. For drugs the picture is different and is covered in section 2.

**India, Turkey, Colombia, Dominican Republic, Barbados.** Broker prices only, no US employer programs found, and the one documented union encounter is the 2006 Blue Ridge Paper case, where the United Steelworkers blocked a worker's company-sponsored trip to India and the company dropped the program [fetched, NYT via Cornell]. That episode is the optics warning.

**Domestic travel programs are the mature version.** Walmart, Lowe's, and McKesson have sent employees to a handful of centers of excellence since 2013 with 100% coverage and travel for the patient and a caregiver, through a third-party administrator that negotiates bundled prices [fetched]; Walmart extended it to most cancers at Mayo Clinic in 2024 [fetched Walmart page; snippet for the expansion]. Surgery Center of Oklahoma posts cash prices: knee $17,679, hip $17,579, one-level lumbar fusion $12,230 outpatient, inguinal hernia $3,870, same price for out-of-state patients [fetched]. Vendors (IndUShealth, Carrum, Transcarent) sell bundles to self-funded plans; fee models are snippet-only.

**Utah's pharmacy tourism program is live** [fetched, pehp.org 2026]: the state employee plan flies a member and a companion to San Diego (for Tijuana) or Vancouver, pays for a night's hotel, and gives up to $750 cash back per trip for a 90-day supply of six named biologics (Enbrel, Orencia, Otezla and others). It began about 2019 at $500 cash under a Utah law requiring the plan to share savings, had saved about $250,000 by early 2020, and about one in 400 eligible members used it [fetched, WBUR 2020].

**Group trips.** The only documented one is the 2019 insulin caravan from Minneapolis to London, Ontario: the group spent about $2,000 on insulin that would have cost almost $24,000 in the US, several pharmacies turned them away before one filled the prescriptions, and no physician travelled with them [fetched, KFF]. No chartered-flight-with-doctor program was found anywhere.

## 2. Where it stops working

- **Cancer: unbuilt, not impossible.** No US employer or insurer sends members abroad for chemotherapy or radiation; NASH announced oncology in 2017 and never launched it. The CDC's 2026 Yellow Book calls oncology tourists "a vulnerable patient population" and warns that treatments abroad "may have no established benefit" [fetched], which is a warning about unproven clinics, not about standard-of-care treatment given at an accredited center. The real constraints are structural: cancer care is a chain (surgery, pathology and molecular testing, a tumor board, systemic therapy over months, imaging every few months, survivorship), and the pieces split differently. Travel-friendly: surgery on the NASH design; a radiation course, now often one to five weeks and for some breast and prostate cases five sessions (NOT VERIFIED here; modern hypofractionation to confirm); second opinions and diagnostics. Relocation-shaped: multi-month infusion cycles, where the member would live near the center for the course, and where the price gap is largest because the drug is the cost (trastuzumab biosimilar in India about $190 to $360 a vial against a US list of about $3,500 [snippets]). Not transferable: emergencies, and the home oncologist's role, which has to be contracted in from the start. The design question is a "treatment residency" product for the subset of members and cancers where the regimen is defined, the center is accredited, records move both ways, and a US oncologist owns the plan at home. The employer pattern today is domestic: a second opinion and plan at a center of excellence, travel paid, then care at home.
- **Bariatric surgery abroad is where the bodies are.** A UC San Diego series of 91 patients treated for complications after bariatric surgery in Mexico (2014 to 2024): 33% leaks, 20% ICU, 3.3% died, mean hospital charges about $193,000 [fetched, Surgical Endoscopy 2025; no denominator]. The 2023 Matamoros fungal meningitis outbreak after epidural anesthesia at two clinics: 185 at risk, 31 US cases, 8 deaths as of July 2023 [fetched, CDC]. Marketing packages in Tijuana run $3,900 to $7,500 [snippet]. Any program would have to look like NASH (US surgeon, accredited hospital, US pre- and post-op) and exclude the clinics that produce these series.
- **GLP-1 arbitrage has mostly closed.** US direct-to-consumer cash prices are now $199 to $449 a month (Novo $349, intro $199; Lilly Zepbound vials $299 to $449) [fetched, BioSpace Dec 2025; therxindex May 2026]; Canadian cash prices for Wegovy are about C$431 to C$493 and Mounjaro C$326 to C$650 [fetched, teletest.ca June 2026]. British Columbia bars sales of GLP-1s to non-residents except in person with a prescription signed by a Canadian prescriber [fetched, BC release July 2025]. Canada's first generic semaglutide for weight loss, Apotex's Sevmia, was authorized June 29, 2026 [fetched, Health Canada]; a Dr. Reddy's generic for diabetes came April 28, 2026 and Sandoz's later [fetched Global News; fetched trade press Sept 2026]; no launch prices yet. Residual savings sit at high doses and in biologics, which is exactly what Utah's program targets.
- **Follow-up.** A foreign physician cannot lawfully run follow-up telemedicine with a New Jersey patient without a New Jersey license (law-firm commentary; patient location controls). The workable design is the same US surgeon operating abroad and seeing the patient at home, or a US physician who owns the aftercare. The CDC says not to fly for ten days after chest or abdominal surgery [fetched].
- **Prevention evidence is narrower than the pitch.** The Diabetes Prevention Program's lifestyle arm cut diabetes incidence 58% in a randomized trial [snippet, NEJM 2002]; Medicare's actuaries found $2,650 saved per enrollee over five quarters in the YMCA program [fetched, CMS 2016]. Broad workplace wellness programs, in the two large randomized trials (BJ's Wholesale; University of Illinois), moved self-reported behaviour and nothing else: no spending, clinical, or absenteeism effect [fetched NBER page; snippets for Health Affairs]. Walking groups lower blood pressure a few points and are cheap [snippet]. Design prevention around the DPP and social connection, not around incentives.

## 3. The group-trip idea, assessed

**Costs.** Broker hourly rates: a nine-seat King Air about $2,000 to $3,000 an hour; a 30-seat Dornier 328 about $4,800 to $6,500 [snippet]. A Teterboro-to-Cancún round trip in a King Air with repositioning is roughly ten to twelve flight hours, so on the order of $22,000 to $27,000 before tax, or $2,500 to $3,400 a seat at eight passengers (inference, NOT VERIFIED; no route quote obtained). Commercial fares are a fraction of that. On-demand charter under FAA Part 135 is capped at 30 seats and fewer than five round trips a week on a city pair before it counts as scheduled service; selling seats individually needs DOT public-charter authority [snippet, FAA and NBAA]. Newark to Montreal is about 370 miles and six to seven hours by road; Toronto about 465 miles and eight to nine hours [snippet].

**Reading.** A charter is a social and symbolic choice, not a cost one; it adds a few thousand dollars per traveller to a trip whose whole point is saving ten to twenty thousand. The version that holds up: a scheduled cohort (say four to eight patients and companions on the same commercial flights, booked together), a US-licensed network physician who operates or at least examines on-site and does the follow-up at home, a block of hotel rooms attached to the hospital, and the week of physical therapy that the Cancún model already includes. The social angle comes from the cohort and the shared recovery week, not the aircraft. A bus to Canada is the pharmacy version of the same thing and is lawful only within the in-person, Canadian-prescriber rules, which means a Canadian clinic visit on arrival; its value has shrunk with US cash prices.

**The work is part of the product.** A member living near a foreign cancer center for a course of treatment, or recovering for a week in a hospital hotel, has time. Some will work their own jobs remotely; the rest could do the organization's administration, scheduling, record-keeping, and peer support from where they are. Togetherness is not a side effect to be hoped for; it is a service line with volunteers as its staff, and it is the one the lodges actually sold. Design it in: a volunteer roster, defined tasks, training, and recognition, so that a trip is also a term of service.

**Optics.** The Steelworkers' 2006 objection ("forced overseas care trips would be the company's next step") is the line to design against: the trip must be voluntary, the member must come out visibly ahead (Ashley: no cost sharing, $5,000, travel paid), a domestic option must always exist, and the organization's own members, not an employer, must be the ones deciding. Puerto Rico and domestic centers of excellence avoid the "sent abroad" framing entirely.

## 4. Rules that shape it

- **For an employer plan**, a travel-surgery benefit is part of a group health plan: it needs plan-document and summary language, ERISA claims procedures, and HIPAA compliance; a badly designed stand-alone benefit violates the ACA at $100 per person per day [fetched, Venable 2022]. Incentives in practice (waived cost sharing, cash) are treated as plan design, not as wellness rewards, though no authority was found saying so (NOT VERIFIED).
- **Tax.** IRS Publication 502 allows lodging at $50 a night per person ($100 with a companion), transportation, and 21 cents a mile, and excludes meals and trips for "general improvement of health" even on a doctor's advice [fetched].
- **For a non-insurance membership**, the question is whether paying for members' surgery is insurance (risk transfer) or a service. New Jersey's health-insurance definition (N.J.S.A. 17B:17-4) was read only in paraphrase; no enacted New Jersey discount-medical-plan statute was found, only unenacted bills; counsel must draw the line. Ithaca's community health fund was challenged by New York's insurance department for the same reason and settled by renaming benefits "grants" (Wikipedia only, NOT VERIFIED).

## 5. Purchasing options, summarized for decision

What the purchasing step of the services page can mean, in rising order of commitment:

1. **Publish prices and steer** (no contracts): the Essex table in dollars, the Oklahoma-style cash benchmarks, Medicare's rates, and a plain recommendation of where a given procedure is cheapest and good. Lawful today.
2. **Negotiate as a coalition, each payer still paying its own claims**: local employers and the township asking hospitals for a Medicare multiple, with the table as evidence. No license. The district can join only through its fund until 2028.
3. **Contract bundles domestically**: Surgery Center of Oklahoma-style cash bundles, a center-of-excellence arrangement for the big-ticket surgeries, Puerto Rico as a near-domestic option to study. Members travel; the organization pays the bundle and travel from a hardship or purchasing fund.
4. **Contract bundles abroad** on the NASH design, if NASH or an equivalent still exists; Costa Rica and Cayman are the candidates with accreditation; Mexico only through a US-surgeon program.
5. **Pharmacy travel** on the Utah model for biologics, with Canada in person and a Canadian prescriber; not for GLP-1s at current US cash prices.
6. **Design later, with care**: a cancer "treatment residency" abroad for defined regimens at accredited centers with a home oncologist contracted in; co-owning or leasing capacity abroad on the Health City (Narayana and Ascension) and NASH (leased floor) models once volume exists; credentialing foreign surgeons directly, with an outcome registry and complication cover, instead of flying US surgeons in for everything.
7. **Not this way**: bariatric packages without the US-surgeon or equivalent structure; any clinic without accreditation and published outcomes.

Decisions still open: whether the organization pays for care at all (which crosses into insurance) or only negotiates and publishes; whether to approach NASH, Health City, and two Puerto Rico hospitals for current prices; whether the first travel cohort is domestic (Oklahoma, a center of excellence) to prove the logistics before anyone leaves the country.

## 6. A pledge launch

**Precedents say the model is old.** Group Health sold $75 lifetime memberships to "a few hundred" people in early 1946 before it had any service, then bought an existing clinic with 10,000 contract enrollees for $190,000, charged a $100 joining fee plus $3 a month per adult, and ended its first year with 1,024 members and 8,500 group enrollees; it also sold $100 "Pioneer Bonds" to members to clear the purchase debt [fetched, HistoryLink]. The REA electric cooperatives collected $5 memberships from a threshold number of households before they could borrow: Platte-Clay in Missouri had 1,367 certificates in hand five months after incorporating and borrowed $487,000 against them [fetched]. The NCUA still charters credit unions this way: field of membership, a member survey, sponsor commitment letters, donated capital, then a business plan; four federal charters in 2024, three in 2025 [fetched]. The all-or-nothing pledge is an assurance contract; Kickstarter and Groupon's tipping point are the modern forms, and the "refund bonus" variant (early pledgers get a bonus if the goal fails) raised success rates by half in lab experiments but has no real-world deployment found [snippet].

**Platform constraints.** Kickstarter's current rules prohibit projects "centered around establishing and providing services" and anything claiming to treat a condition [fetched copy of May 2026 rules]; it is unusable for a care membership. Card authorizations last seven days (thirty with extended authorization, which excludes healthcare transactions) [fetched, Stripe], so a months-long pledge cannot be a hold. The workable mechanics are a saved payment method plus a signed commitment, charged only when the goal is met, or a true escrow account with refund on failure. Open Collective is a transparent ledger, not an escrow, and its US fiscal host dissolved at the end of 2024 [fetched; snippet].

**New Jersey law touchpoints, all for counsel.**
- The Charitable Registration and Investigation Act makes it unlawful to solicit before the Attorney General has reviewed the registration, with a $10,000 all-volunteer exemption and a 30-day register-after rule; "contribution" includes a pledge but excludes "bona fide fees, dues or assessments paid by members provided that membership is not conferred solely as consideration" for a donation [fetched]. Pledges that are genuine dues for defined services may sit outside the Act; donation tiers labelled "membership" do not.
- New Jersey has no direct-primary-care statute and no pending bill found; a provision in the organized-delivery-system law appears to exempt capitation from "financial risk" but is untested [fetched, DPC Frontier, undated]. The state's own school employee plan already offers a zero-fee direct primary care benefit through Marathon Health, with an office in West Orange [fetched]. Corporate practice of medicine is enforced, so a lay member-owned entity cannot simply employ physicians.
- No general cooperative corporation statute; groups incorporate under the nonprofit (15A) or business (14A) corporation acts; an 1881 workers' cooperative law exists and a modernization bill, S120, is stuck in committee [fetched]. Federal tax: section 501(m) denies exemption where "commercial-type insurance" is a substantial activity; Ithaca Health Alliance is a 501(c)(3), Freelancers Union a 501(c)(4) [fetched; snippets]. No on-point ruling for a primary-care membership cooperative.

**Calendar.** Get Covered NJ open enrollment for 2027 runs November 1, 2026 to January 31, 2027 [fetched]; the school employees' plan enrolls in the last week of October for a January 1 start [snippet]; 77% of employers open enrollment in October or November [fetched]. An October 1, 2027 pledge deadline gives every household a yes or no before it picks 2028 coverage. The district contract's end date was not found and is not asserted.

**Numbers.** Direct primary care averages $98.46 a month nationally, lower at larger practices [snippet, DPC Alliance 2026]. At $100 a month: 1,000 members is $1.2 million a year, 3,000 is $3.6 million, 5,000 is $6 million (computed). Group Health reached 1,024 members in its first year and 2,811 in its second; Ithaca's fund reached about 700 to 1,000 members over a decade at $100 a year.

**Disclosures a campaign should carry** (inference from Kickstarter's rules and FTC v. Chevalier): who the organizers are; exactly what a pledge buys; goals, deadline, and what failure means; that nothing is charged until the goal is met and how the money is held; refund policy; a risks section covering regulatory status, physician recruitment, and premises; that it is not insurance and does not replace coverage; registration status; and reporting commitments.
