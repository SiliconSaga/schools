# How Services Could Work

[How the Pay Works](/ledger/pay) and [How the Health Benefits Work](/ledger/benefits) describe the system the district and its employees are inside. This page asks a different question, one residents have started asking out loud: if the premiums rise 11% to 27% a year and the state plan behind them is losing members, is there anything a town can build for itself? It is the ledger keeper's assessment, not record, and it is marked as such throughout. Where it describes what other communities did, every claim is linked to a source. Where it describes what West Orange could do, it says so. Nothing here is legal or financial advice, and nothing here is a proposal to the Board; the Board cannot do most of it, which is rather the point.

The short answer is that communities have built their own health care, banking, and utilities before, under their own control. Some of what they built, the credit unions and the electric cooperatives, is still theirs; the health care they built was, in every case this page could find, bought, converted, or legislated away. The history says what works and what to lock the doors against. The current law says where a town can start without asking anyone's permission: on the prices, on primary care, and on how hard decisions get made.

## What the catastrophic question taught

The [benefits page](/ledger/benefits#going-without-insurance-except-for-catastrophe) worked through the idea of dropping insurance for everything but catastrophe. For one healthy person it is legal and getting cheaper. For a family or an older worker it fails, and for an employer it is not allowed. But the arithmetic taught something more useful than that: a catastrophic plan is not a different system, it is the same system with the first $12,000 moved onto the household. In New Jersey's 2026 individual market the cheapest catastrophic plan costs about a quarter less than the cheapest bronze plan for a 40-year-old and about a third less than the cheapest silver, not four-fifths less, because most of what a plan pays is hospital care, surgery, cancer, and specialty drugs, and a catastrophic plan still pays those. The district's own rate sheet says the same thing: its high-deductible plan costs $1,325 a month against $1,391 for the Educators plan, a 5% difference ([June 16, 2026 minutes, pp. 23-25](https://resources.finalsite.net/images/v1784732860/woboeorg/jryunce7m2qtccixad76/June162026MINUTES.pdf)). Moving the deductible barely moves the premium because the deductible was never where the money was.

The money is in three places. **Prices**, which only a purchaser with leverage can touch. **Administration**, which the most-cited study on the subject put at 34.2% of United States health spending in 2017 against 17.0% in Canada ([Himmelstein, Campbell, and Woolhandler, Annals of Internal Medicine, January 2020](https://pnhp.org/news/landmark-administrative-waste-study-updated/); a narrower federal measure counting only insurers' and government's own overhead puts it at 7.0% of national spending in 2024, per [Peterson-KFF](https://www.healthsystemtracker.org/chart-collection/u-s-spending-healthcare-changed-time/), and New Jersey's own cost-growth report puts the net cost of private insurance at 6.1% of the state's health spending in 2023, per the [HART Program report](https://www.nj.gov/health/about/documents/ohcat/NJHARTProgramCostGrowthBenchmarkPerformanceYear1Report_2022-2023.pdf)). And **the sickest few**, whose care is most of any pool's claims and the moral core of the whole thing. A design that aims at those three, rather than at the insurance product, is a different project from the one about deductibles. It is also the project this country has run before.

## This has been done before

**Lodge practice.** Before employer insurance existed, Americans bought their doctors through fraternal societies. By 1920 at least three in ten adult men belonged to one, about 18 million people ([Beito, "Lodge Doctors and the Poor," 1994](https://fee.org/articles/lodge-doctors-and-the-poor/), drawing on his book [From Mutual Aid to the Welfare State](https://cdn.mises.org/qjae4_4_6.pdf), University of North Carolina Press, 2000; also the [Social Welfare History Project](https://socialwelfare.library.vcu.edu/organizations/reciprocal-aid-fraternalism-and-early-social-welfare-history/)). A lodge contracted a physician at an annual salary set by its membership, and "for one dollar a year, a member and immediate family could receive basic medical services (including minor surgery)"; two dollars a year was "roughly equivalent to a day's wage for a laborer." The medical journals of the day confirm the price from the other side: a 1904 report in the Journal of the American Medical Association on St. Cloud, Minnesota, where "at least one-sixth of the population" was covered, gives the fee as "$1.00 (or $2.00 if the whole family is included) per annum" ([JAMA, May 21, 1904](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/459645)). The lodge was the sports league, the congregation, and the block association of its day, and the doctor's contract was one of the things it did.

**Credit unions.** The first in the country opened in the parlor of a parishioner's house in Manchester, New Hampshire, on November 24, 1908, organized by the pastor of a French-Canadian parish ([St. Mary's Bank](https://www.stmarysbank.com/nav/about-us/history); [New England Historical Society](https://newenglandhistoricalsociety.com/first-u-s-credit-union-opens-in-manchestern-h/)). Massachusetts passed the first general credit union law in 1909 and Congress the Federal Credit Union Act in 1934 ([NCUA timeline](https://ncua.gov/about/historical-timeline)). As of June 30, 2026 there were 4,214 federally insured credit unions with 146.1 million members and $2.50 trillion in assets ([NCUA, 2026 Q2](https://ncua.gov/files/publications/analysis/quarterly-data-summary-2026-Q2.pdf)). Federal law lets a credit union's membership be "persons or organizations within a well-defined local community, neighborhood, or rural district" ([12 U.S.C. 1759(b)(3)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/12/1759)), which is to say a town.

**Prepaid care.** Kaiser Permanente began in 1933 as a young doctor's hospital in the California desert for men building an aqueduct, who "voluntarily prepaid a premium of 5 cents per day from their paycheck"; the same model followed Kaiser's workers to Grand Coulee Dam and the wartime shipyards, and on July 21, 1945 "the Permanente Health Plan officially opened to the public" ([Kaiser Permanente history](https://about.kaiserpermanente.org/who-we-are/our-history/how-it-all-started)). Group Health Cooperative of Puget Sound was incorporated by Seattle machinists, Grange members, and co-op organizers on December 22, 1945, as "a non-profit corporation with a member-elected board," bought a clinic and hospital for $190,000 with its members' money, and "formally began caring for its few hundred original members on January 1, 1947" ([HistoryLink essays 2747](https://historylink.org/File/2747) and [7546](https://www.historylink.org/File/7546)).

**Utilities and banking.** When nine in ten farms had no electricity and the investor-owned utilities would not run the lines, the Rural Electrification Act of 1936 lent the money to member-owned cooperatives instead; today 890 of them serve an estimated 42 million people and own about 42% of the nation's distribution lines ([NRECA history](https://www.electric.coop/our-organization/history); [fact sheet, April 2026](https://www.cooperative.com/programs-services/bts/Documents/Data/Electric-Co-op-Fact-Sheet.pdf)). The Post Office ran savings accounts from 1911 to 1967, paying 2% and "peaking in 1947 at almost $3.4 billion, with more than four million depositors using 8,141 postal units" ([USPS Historian](https://about.usps.com/who/profile/history/pdf/postal-savings-system.pdf)).

None of these waited for a national solution to health care or banking. The lodges, the credit unions, Kaiser, and Group Health began as practice, and the laws that recognized them came afterward; the electric cooperatives are the exception, created and financed by a federal law once the private utilities had declined the work. Either way, each started with a few hundred people and one narrow service.

## And undone

The health care examples were taken apart, and the ways it happened are the design constraints for anything built now.

**Lodge practice was killed by organized medicine.** "By the 1910s, doctors had launched an all-out war against lodge practice," Beito writes; "medical associations imposed a range of sanctions against lodge doctors, including expulsion from the association and denial of hospital facilities," and in some places "campaigns were organized to deny patient care, even in emergencies, to members of offending lodges" ([Beito, 1994](https://fee.org/articles/lodge-doctors-and-the-poor/)). The journals called it the "lodge practice evil" ([JAMA, 1904](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/459645)), and county societies passed resolutions "pledging its members to have no professional intercourse with lodge-contract physicians" ([JAMA, January 20, 1906](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/articlepdf/455990/jama_xlvi_3_010.pdf)). By the end of the 1930s it "had virtually disappeared." What replaced it was not chosen by anyone: wartime wage controls in 1942 exempted "insurance and pension benefits," a 1943 ruling made the employer's premium tax-free to the worker, and the 1954 tax code made that permanent, so coverage followed the job ([Congressional Research Service, RL34767](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/RL34767.html); enrollment went from 20.7 million in 1940 to 142.3 million in 1950, per [EH.net](https://eh.net/encyclopedia/health-insurance-in-the-united-states/)).

**Group Health was sold.** On March 12, 2016 its members voted 8,824 to 1,585 to be acquired by Kaiser Permanente for $1.8 billion, clearing the two-thirds the bylaws required ([KOMO News](https://komonews.com/news/local/group-health-members-ok-acquisition-by-kaiser-permanente)); the deal closed in February 2017 and the money went into a foundation ([Lund Report](https://www.thelundreport.org/content/kaiser-permanente-completes-18b-acquisition-group-health); [Grantmakers in Health](https://www.gih.org/?p=4794)). Kaiser is itself not-for-profit and well regarded, with 12.9 million members ([Kaiser Permanente fast facts](https://about.kaiserpermanente.org/who-we-are/fast-facts)), so what was lost was not the care but the ownership: after seventy years the people of Puget Sound no longer own their health system or elect its board, and the price of it went to a foundation rather than back to the members. A sale to a good buyer is still a sale, and it is final.

**The Blues converted.** The Blue Cross plans began as nonprofits run by hospitals and doctors, charged everyone in a community the same rate, and "originally offered last-resort insurance as part of [their] corporate mission," which is why states exempted them from premium taxes; managed care in the 1980s ended those practices, and the plans themselves then changed hands ([Hall and Conover, Milbank Quarterly, 2003](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2690248/)). Blue Cross of California became the for-profit WellPoint in the 1990s and, "under pressure from regulators," funded two foundations "with stock valued at more than $3 billion"; Blue Cross plans in 13 other states converted between 1993 and 2002, and "one clear effect of conversion is to increase profit incentives" ([Hall and Conover, Milbank Quarterly, 2003](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2690248/)). New York's Empire conversion law was "passed by the legislature in the middle of the night in January 2002 without any public hearings" ([Consumers Union](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/press_release/suit-challenging-ny-law-allowing-insurer-to-go-for-profit-advances)). The mutual life insurers went the same way around 2000: Metropolitan Life "converted from a mutual life insurance company to a stock life insurance company" on April 7, 2000, and Prudential on December 18, 2001 ([MetLife 10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001099219/000095012300010601/y42537e10-q.txt); [Prudential 10-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1137774/000119312504038160/d10k.htm)).

**The Affordable Care Act's cooperatives were starved.** The 2010 law created a new kind of insurer, the Consumer Operated and Oriented Plan: a nonprofit governed by its own members, started with federal loans, meant to give the new exchanges a member-owned alternative to the commercial carriers. Twenty-three opened. A rider in the December 2014 spending bill barred the government from paying their promised loss-sharing from other funds, and the program paid 12.6 cents on the dollar for 2014 ([CMS memo, October 1, 2015](https://www.kff.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2015/10/riskcorridorspaymentprorationratefor2014.pdf)). New Jersey's, Health Republic Insurance of New Jersey, "consented to be placed in rehabilitation due to its deteriorating financial condition" on September 12, 2016, with 26,000 individual and 9,000 small-group members and a $46.3 million bill under the law's risk-adjustment formula ([Department of Banking and Insurance](https://nj.gov/dobi/pressreleases/pr160912.html)). By 2022 three of the 23 survived ([Adashi, O'Mahony, and Cohen, 2022](https://hls.harvard.edu/bibliography/and-then-there-were-three-the-decimation-of-the-affordable-care-act-aca-co-ops)). On April 27, 2020 the Supreme Court ruled 8-1 that the government had owed the money all along, more than $12 billion, "a principle as old as the Nation itself: The Government should honor its obligations" ([Maine Community Health Options v. United States](https://www.scotusblog.com/case-files/cases/maine-community-health-options-v-united-states); [AHLA summary](https://www.americanhealthlaw.org/content-library/health-law-weekly/article/1adfaeca-a4d3-457b-8252-f7cc87cf3f73/supreme-court-finds-federal-government-obligated-t)). Most of the cooperatives, New Jersey's among them, were dead by then.

**What the history teaches, in three rules.**

1. **Lock the assets.** A community-owned body must not be sellable or convertible for private gain. Britain writes this into law: every community interest company has "a compulsory Asset Lock that cannot be removed," and assets may leave only at full market value or to another asset-locked body ([UK CIC Regulator](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/community-interest-companies-how-to-form-a-cic/community-interest-companies-guidance-chapters)). The nearest thing in United States law is the rule that a charity's assets must be "dedicated to an exempt purpose" and may not go to members on dissolution ([Treas. Reg. 1.501(c)(3)-1(b)(4)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.501(c)(3)-1)), which is weaker, so the lock has to be written into the founding documents and the governance has to be unable to vote it away. Group Health's members could, and did.
2. **Govern by members, and expect to be fought.** Group Health's doctors were refused membership in the county medical society until the state supreme court "put it on probation for three years" in 1951 ([HistoryLink](https://www.historylink.org/File/7546)). The lodges were boycotted. Anything that lowers prices should expect opposition from the people who receive them.
3. **Depend on no promise a future legislature can cancel.** The cooperatives died of a rider. Whatever a town builds has to stand on its own members' money.

## Shining a light on the prices

Of the three places the money sits, prices are the one a community can look at today without building anything, because federal law now requires hospitals and insurers to publish them. This is the same work the Ledger does with the Board's budget, pointed at the hospitals.

**What the law requires.** Since January 1, 2021 every hospital must post a machine-readable file of its standard charges, including the price it has negotiated with each insurer and its cash price, plus a consumer display of 300 "shoppable" services ([CMS](https://www.cms.gov/priorities/key-initiatives/hospital-price-transparency); [45 CFR 180.50](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/180.50)). Insurers have had to publish their negotiated in-network rates since July 2022 and run a price-comparison tool for members since 2023 ([CMS fact sheet](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/transparency-coverage-final-rule-fact-sheet-cms-9915-f)). The files were long, inconsistent, and often filled with percentages instead of dollars. An executive order of February 25, 2025 demanded "the actual prices of items and services, not estimates" ([Executive Order 14221](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/DCPD-202500302/html/DCPD-202500302.htm)). Since January 1, 2026, wherever a hospital's negotiated price with an insurer is a percentage or a formula rather than a dollar figure, it must also publish the median and the 10th and 90th percentile of what that insurer actually paid, drawn from remittance data, and every file must carry an attestation signed by a senior officer; enforcement began April 1, 2026 ([CMS fact sheet, November 21, 2025](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/cy-2026-opps-ambulatory-surgical-center-final-rule-hospital-price-transparency-policy-changes)). Between April and early June 2026, 519 hospitals received noncompliance letters ([Forvis Mazars](https://www.forvismazars.us/forsights/2026/06/price-transparency-enforcement-hhs-cms-reaffirm-focus)), and on October 5, 2026 the Federal Trade Commission warned 24 of the largest health care companies that "the CMS rules do not create a safe harbor from FTC Act liability" for incomplete price disclosures ([FTC](https://ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/10/ftc-issues-letters-warning-hospitals-against-deceptive-pricing-practices)). The insurers' files were slimmed by a rule finalized the same day ([CMS fact sheet, October 5, 2026](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/transparency-coverage-final-rules-cms-9882-f)). Compliance is still partial: the advocacy group that audits the files found 49.4% of 2,000 hospitals fully compliant in September 2026, its highest reading yet ([Patient Rights Advocate](https://www.patientrightsadvocate.org/eighth-annual-hospital-price-transparency-compliance-report-september-2026)); its last New Jersey figure, from early 2024, was 41% ([The Record, February 5, 2024](https://www.yahoo.com/news/battle-over-nj-hospital-prices-093047354.html)). No New Jersey hospital appears on the federal penalty list ([CMS enforcement actions](https://www.cms.gov/priorities/key-initiatives/hospital-price-transparency/enforcement-actions)).

**Who already publishes it in readable form.** Several groups do, none of them with New Jersey in mind. For a patient: [Turquoise Health](https://turquoise.health/patients) and Trilliant Health's [Oria](https://oria-data.trillianthealth.com/) search the hospitals' own files by procedure and location; [FAIR Health Consumer](https://www.fairhealthconsumer.org/), a nonprofit, estimates from claims data by ZIP code. For an analyst: [Sage Transparency](https://employerptp.org/sage-transparency/), built by an Indiana employers' coalition with the RAND Corporation's price studies, shows each hospital's prices as a percentage of Medicare and is free; Patient Rights Advocate keeps a [catalog of every hospital's raw file](https://www.patientrightsadvocate.org/blog/hospitalpricefilesfinder); a community database on [DoltHub](http://www.dolthub.com/blog/2025-01-28-state-of-hospital-price-transparency-data/) gathered some 300 million prices in 2022 and has not been updated since 2023. Colorado's state Medicaid agency, with a nonprofit data center, built a public price tool and a cleaned database from its hospitals' files ([CIVHC](https://civhc.org/hospital-price-transparency-database/)); no New Jersey equivalent was found. The state hospital association launched [NJ Hospital Price Compare](https://www.njhospitalpricecompare.com/) in 2007 to show list charges by diagnosis; in October 2026 the site did not answer and the association's page describing it had been removed, so whether it still exists is one of the open questions below. New Jersey has no all-payer claims database (a bill to create one, [A5540](https://pub.njleg.gov/Bills/2026/A6000/5540_I1.HTM), was introduced September 24, 2026); a bill to give the state enforcement power and bar noncompliant hospitals from collecting medical debt ([S3012](https://pub.njleg.gov/Bills/2026/S3500/3012_I1.HTM)) and one to ban facility fees at hospital-owned outpatient offices ([S3409](https://pub.njleg.gov/Bills/2026/S3500/3409_I1.HTM)) are in committee. What the state does publish is the cost-growth benchmark report: per-person spending rose 6.1% from 2022 to 2023 against a 3.5% target, and in the commercial market "price increases rather than utilization" drove the growth ([Department of Health, January 9, 2026](https://www.nj.gov/health/news/2026/approved/20260109c.shtml)).

**What our hospitals charge.** Nobody has to wait for a tool to see the shape of it. In September 2025 researchers at Brown University's School of Public Health, using the RAND study and a national hospital cost tool, wrote that "New Jersey has the twentieth highest hospital facility prices in the nation" and that in 2022 "New Jersey hospitals charged commercial insurers prices that averaged 267% of Medicare rates." Their table gives, hospital by hospital, the price New Jersey's state employees paid relative to Medicare for the same services ([Brown CAHPR memo, September 4, 2025](https://cahpr.sph.brown.edu/sites/default/files/documents/Public%20Comments/09.04.2025%20CAHPR%20RBP%20Memo%20for%20New%20Jersey.pdf), Table 1). For the acute-care hospitals in Essex County:

| Hospital | Inpatient, % of Medicare | Outpatient, % of Medicare |
|---|---|---|
| Cooperman Barnabas Medical Center, Livingston | not reported | 279% |
| Hackensack Meridian Mountainside Medical Center, Montclair | 257% | 282% |
| Clara Maass Medical Center, Belleville | 236% | 257% |
| Newark Beth Israel Medical Center | 221% | 349% |
| Saint Michael's Medical Center, Newark | not reported | 202% |
| East Orange General Hospital (now CareWell Health) | not reported | 214% |
| University Hospital, Newark (public) | 103% | 175% |

Cooperman Barnabas, Clara Maass, and Newark Beth Israel belong to RWJBarnabas Health, whose [charges page](https://www.rwjbh.org/billing/charges) hosts their files; Mountainside is "a joint venture between Hackensack Meridian Health and Ardent Healthcare," a Tennessee for-profit ([HMH directory](https://hmhmaestro.org/en/helpful-tools/hospital-directory/mountainside-medical-center)); University Hospital is the state's public academic medical center and posts [its file](https://www.uhnj.org/patients-visitors/transparency-in-billing/hospital-charges/) on its own site. The Brown researchers estimated that capping the state employee plan's hospital payments at 200% of Medicare would save it about $433 million a year, $15 million of it at Cooperman Barnabas alone, with the hospitals' overall operating margins falling from 9.3% to 8.1%. The state's public-employee unions have proposed exactly that cap, twice; it failed in June 2025 for want of votes ([League of Municipalities](https://njlm.org/CivicAlerts.asp?AID=3453)) and was reintroduced for 2027 ([Resolution 2026-6](https://www.nj.gov/treasury/pensions/documents/pdf/2026-6-LaborResolution-RBPINNPilot.pdf)). Montana's state plan did it in 2016 and saved $47.8 million in three years ([NASHP](https://nashp.org/independent-analysis-estimating-the-impact-of-reference-based-hospital-pricing-on-the-montana-state-employee-plan/)); Oregon's did it by statute in 2017, saved $107.5 million in 27 months, and a December 2025 study found the hospitals' finances unchanged ([Milbank](https://milbank.org/publications/how-payment-caps-can-reduce-hospital-prices-and-spending-lessons-from-the-oregon-state-employee-plan); [Brown](https://sph.brown.edu/news/2025-12-02/hospital-price-caps)).

**What a town could do with this.** Publish the table above every year from the hospitals' own files, in dollars, for the hundred things families actually buy: a birth, an MRI, a child's broken arm, a colonoscopy, an emergency visit that becomes an observation stay. Say which insurer pays what at which hospital, and which hospital charges a cash price below its insured price. It costs nothing but attention and it is exactly the kind of record this ledger keeps for the Board. The School Health Insurance Fund the district buys through has 111 member districts ([Office of the State Comptroller, September 2025](https://www.nj.gov/comptroller/library/reports/HIF/2025-09-09_hif_appendix_a.pdf)), and no record found shows it or any of the state's ten school and municipal health funds negotiating hospital prices against a Medicare benchmark. Whether SHIF has asked Aetna for those prices, and what it was told, is a question for the Board's next renewal.

## A credit union for care

Here is the part that is the keeper's own, and it borrows the credit union's shape rather than the insurer's: one membership, member governance with the three locks above, and separately regulated service lines underneath, added one at a time as each becomes viable. Nobody is proposing to buy a hospital. The order matters, because the law allows some lines today and not others.

**First, the record.** The price table, published and kept. Already lawful, already possible.

**Second, primary care.** A nonprofit direct primary care practice with the asset lock, selling memberships to households and blocks to employers. A direct practice charges a flat monthly fee and bills no insurer; the national average fee is about $99 a month and a full-time physician carries a panel of roughly 400 to 700 patients ([DPC Alliance survey, 2026](https://blog.hint.com/state-of-dpc-2026-key-takeaways-from-the-dpc-alliances-physician-survey); [AAFP](https://www.aafp.org/family-physician/practice-and-career/delivery-payment-models/direct-primary-care.html)). There is one in West Orange already, listed at $99 a month for an adult and $39 for a child ([directory listing](https://mapper.dpcfrontier.com/practice/qgadhgwkdtep); not verified by this ledger), and the state's own employee plans have offered a direct primary care membership at no fee since a 2016 pilot ([Division of Pensions and Benefits](https://www.nj.gov/treasury/pensions/dpcmh.shtml)). Since January 2026 the fee can be paid from a health savings account alongside a bronze or catastrophic plan ([IRS Notice 2026-05](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/treasury-irs-provide-guidance-on-new-tax-benefits-for-health-savings-account-participants-under-the-one-big-beautiful-bill)). Primary care is the layer where a voluntary community pool works, because it is a service rather than insurance: a sick member costs a direct practice more visits, not more money. It is also the layer that decides where everything downstream happens. The scale is not frightening:

| Members | Fee | Revenue a year | Full-time clinicians |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000 | $99 | about $1.2 million | 2 |
| 5,000 | $99 | about $5.9 million | 8 |
| 20,000 | $99 | about $24 million | 33 |

Twenty thousand is two in five residents of a township of 48,843 ([2020 census](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Orange,_New_Jersey)). It is a real primary care system owned by its patients, reachable with memberships and employer blocks. For the district, buying a block for its employees before 2028 raises the Chapter 44 question the [benefits page](/ledger/benefits#the-law-and-what-changes-at-the-end-of-2027) leaves open, and after 2027 it is a bargaining subject.

A primary care membership is not comprehensive coverage, and it should never be sold as one; a member still needs a plan for the hospital, the specialist, and the expensive drug. The point of the credit union's shape is what comes next. The same membership and the same locked assets can add service lines as each becomes lawful and affordable: specialist visits and imaging at prices negotiated by the purchasing step below, prescriptions through a pharmacy that publishes its markup, and eventually a hospital contract priced on a Medicare multiple. Each line should stand as its own legal entity with its own reserves where the law allows, answering to its own regulator where one applies, so that a failure in one is less likely to pull down the others; separate books alone do not do that, and a shared membership cannot be allowed to mean shared liability. The member still sees one organization and casts one vote. Comprehensive care at affordable prices is the destination; the credit union's lesson is that it is reached one honest service at a time, not promised whole on the first day.

**Third, purchasing.** Bring the self-funded health plans of local employers, public and private, to the hospitals together and pay on a Medicare multiple, as Montana and Oregon do, with the price table as the evidence. A coalition that only negotiates prices, each employer still paying its own claims, pools no risk and needs no license. Pooling the claims is a different thing: for private employers it becomes a multiple-employer welfare arrangement that New Jersey requires to register, while public bodies pool through joint insurance funds such as SHIF under their own statute. Either way that is the fifth step below, not this one. A single family cannot negotiate with RWJBarnabas. A coalition of a town's employers, a school fund with 111 districts, or a county can. Under the law as it stands the Board can join such a coalition only through its fund or, after 2027, through its contract; the township and local businesses can do it now.

**Fourth, the hard decisions**, which get their own section below.

**Fifth and last, insurance itself.** Promising to pay whatever a member's care costs comes last, because the law makes it the hardest step and because it is where the cooperatives above lost. Three obstacles, plainly. Individual policies are pooled statewide by federal rule, so a group of healthy West Orange households buying catastrophic plans together would lower the state's average cost, not its own. An organization that pays claims itself needs a license, reserves, stop-loss cover for the worst cases, and enough members that one bad year cannot empty the reserves; how many that is, an actuary would have to say, not this page. A single employer funding its own plan answers to federal law alone; several private employers pooling claims form a self-funded multiple-employer welfare arrangement, which New Jersey has regulated and required to register with the state since 2004 ([Department of Banking and Insurance](https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_insurance/mewaapps.htm); N.J.A.C. 11:4-56); public bodies have their joint insurance funds. And the credit union analogy breaks here: federal law gives credit unions a charter for "a well-defined local community," and nothing like it exists for a community health plan, as far as this page can find. Creating one is a job for Trenton. The shortcut on offer, the health care sharing ministry, sits outside insurance law with no obligation to pay claims; such ministries have at least 1.5 million members nationally ([Commonwealth Fund and Georgetown](https://chir.georgetown.edu/health-care-sharing-ministries-leave-consumers-with-unpaid-medical-claims/)), a New Jersey bill to widen them, [S3634](https://pub.njleg.gov/Bills/2026/S4000/3634_I1.HTM), is in committee, and their unpaid claims are the record of what "no insurance" looks like without the three locks.

One town need not do this alone. The lodges federated; the credit unions formed leagues; the electric cooperatives share a national association. Neighboring towns and districts with the same hospitals and the same premiums are the natural partners, and a membership organization in one town is a template for the next.

## Deciding hard cases in the open

Every system that pays for care decides who gets what. The question is whether the decision is visible. Right now it is not, and it is increasingly automated.

**What insurers already do.** In 2023 ProPublica reported that "over a period of two months last year, Cigna doctors denied over 300,000 requests for payments ... spending an average of 1.2 seconds on each case" using a system called PXDX ([ProPublica, March 25, 2023](https://www.propublica.org/article/cigna-pxdx-medical-health-insurance-rejection-claims)); a class action on those facts is in discovery, with class certification argued this fall ([scheduling report](https://litigationtracker.law.georgetown.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/Kisting-Leung-et-al-v.-Cigna_2025.05.23_JOINT-SCHEDULING-REPORT.pdf)). Families of Medicare Advantage patients sued UnitedHealth in 2023 over a tool called nH Predict, alleging that "over 90 percent of patient claim denials are reversed" on appeal and that only about 0.2% of patients appeal; the company denies using the tool to decide claims. In February 2025 a federal judge let the contract claims proceed, and in March 2026 ordered the company to produce its documents on the tool going back to 2017 ([amended complaint](https://litigationtracker.law.georgetown.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Lokken_2024.04.05_AMENDED-COMPLAINT.pdf); [opinion, February 13, 2025](https://litigationtracker.law.georgetown.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Lokken_2025.02.13_MEMORANDUM-OPINION-AND-ORDER.pdf); [discovery order, reported April 2026](https://www.afslaw.com/perspectives/alerts/federal-court-orders-broad-discovery-against-uhc-ai-coverage-denial-lawsuit)). A Senate investigation found UnitedHealthcare's denial rate for post-hospital care went from 10.9% in 2020 to 22.7% in 2022 and that in December 2022 a company working group met "to explore how to use AI and 'machine learning'" in predicting denials ([Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, October 17, 2024](https://www.blumenthal.senate.gov/newsroom/press/release/senate-permanent-subcommittee-on-investigations-releases-majority-staff-report-exposing-medicare-advantage-insurers-refusal-of-care-for-vulnerable-seniors)). Medicare told its plans in February 2024 that an algorithm's prediction "alone cannot be used as the basis to terminate post-acute care services" ([CMS FAQ](https://www.aamc.org/media/74896/download?attachment)). California made it law on January 1, 2025 that an algorithm "shall not deny, delay, or modify health care services based, in whole or in part, on medical necessity" and that a physician must make that call ([SB 1120](https://ncoil.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/UTF-820230SB1120_90-2.pdf)); about a dozen states have followed in 2025 and 2026 ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/05/states-continue-efforts-to-regulate-ai-in-healthcare)). New Jersey has not; its only bill on algorithmic claim denials covers home, auto, and flood insurance ([A5494](https://pub.njleg.gov/Bills/2026/A5500/5494_I1.HTM)).

So the automation is already here, built by distant companies to protect their margins, and the law is scrambling to require that a human be in the room. The honest question for a community is not whether to use these tools. It is who they work for and what they are allowed to decide.

**A model from the courthouse.** New Jersey already runs a system that answers that question tolerably well, for a pool far larger than any health membership: jury selection. The courts build one list by merging "registered voters, licensed drivers, filers of State gross income tax returns" and homestead applicants, and "prospective jurors are randomly selected by computer" ([New Jersey Courts](https://www.njcourts.gov/jurors/faq); [N.J.S.A. 2B:20-2](https://pub.njleg.gov/bills/2020/A4500/4275_I1.HTM)). Who is eligible is set by statute, a citizen of 18 or older who lives in the county, reads English, can serve, and has no disqualifying conviction; the part that matters here is not who is eligible but how the people who actually serve are chosen from among them: by a neutral draw under a rule published in advance. The computer does not decide anything about a person's life; it draws names. Anyone for whom service would be a severe hardship asks to be excused, on grounds the statute lists, and "may speak with the trial judge" directly. Four hundred people can wait in a room all morning and nobody thinks the draw was unfair, because the rule was public before the draw, the machine was neutral, and the hard case went to a human who could hear it (although a streamlined system could perhaps trim the wait and the uncertainty just a bit).

That is the design. The criteria are published before any case is considered. The machine assembles the record: the diagnosis, the evidence, the price, the alternatives, what the published rule says. A small panel of members, drawn at random for a fixed term, decides, in minutes rather than hours, because the record is complete; a panel rather than one person, so that no single reviewer's habit of saying yes or of saying no becomes the rule. A different panel hears the appeal. The outcomes are published in aggregate, including the refusals. The machine is the preparer, never the decider, and the people who decide are members, not employees of the payer.

**It has been done in public before.** Britain's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence has since 1999 decided in the open which treatments the health service will pay for, publishing draft guidance for comment and allowing appeals to a panel; about a third of appeals succeed, and the threshold it uses, which stood at £20,000 to £30,000 per quality-adjusted year of life from 1999, rises to £25,000 to £35,000 for medicines considered from April 2026 ([Rawlins, Journal of the Royal Society of Medicine, 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4480561); [Pharmaceutical Journal, December 2, 2025](https://pharmaceutical-journal.com/article/news/nice-cost-effectiveness-thresholds-increased-as-uk-strikes-trade-deal-with-united-states)). Oregon's Medicaid program has since 1994 covered care from a ranked public list, now 660 lines of which the first 469 are covered, reviewed every two years in open meetings ([Oregon Health Authority](https://www.oregon.gov/oha/HPA/DSI-HERC/Pages/Prioritized-List-Overview.aspx); [Bodenheimer, New England Journal of Medicine, 1997](https://facultystaff.richmond.edu/~bmayes/pdf/oregon_i.pdf)). Neither is a comfortable example. NICE has been called a rationing board, and worse, for as long as it has existed; the health service around it carries a waiting list of more than seven million cases ([BMA backlog analysis](https://www.bma.org.uk/advice-and-support/nhs-delivery-and-workforce/pressures/nhs-backlog-data-analysis)); and critics said the 2026 threshold change was set by a trade deal rather than by evidence ([Science Media Centre](https://www.sciencemediacentre.org/expert-reaction-to-announcement-on-uk-us-pharmaceuticals-deal-and-changes-to-nices-cost-effectiveness-thresholds/)). Anyone proposing open decisions here should expect the words "death panel." The answer is that every payer already decides what it will not cover; the only choice is whether the deciding is visible and appealable, and the price of doing it that way is lower than the price of 1.2 seconds.

**The weight-loss drugs are the test case.** The spring essay on [the limits of local self-reliance](/bigger-picture-void-limits) called this the immortality-pill problem: a drug too good and too expensive for everyone to have now. The state plan's own actuaries found weight-loss drugs to be 22% of what the plan pays for prescriptions ([benefits page](/ledger/benefits#the-state-plan-and-the-pool)). A membership organization that excluded them for weight loss, as North Carolina's state plan did, would need the open process above for the people who need them medically, a hardship fund, and time: Novo Nordisk's filings put the compound patent expiry for semaglutide in the United States at 2032 ([Form 20-F, 2024](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/353278/000162828025003920/nvo-20241231.htm)), while Canada approved its first generic for weight loss on June 29, 2026 ([Health Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/news/2026/06/canada-approves-first-generic-semaglutide-for-weight-loss.html)). The border is not a shortcut: "in most circumstances, it is illegal for individuals to import drugs or devices into the U.S. for personal use," with discretion for about a three-month supply of a drug for a serious condition that cannot be had here ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/industry/import-basics/personal-importation)), and the one state program to import from Canada, Florida's, was authorized in January 2024 and has had its authorization extended four times with no shipment reported ([FDA, Section 804 program](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/importation-program-under-section-804-fdc-act/section-804-importation-program-policies-and-authorizations)). What a membership can do about drug prices on its own is narrower: buy generics from a pharmacy that sells at cost plus a published markup, as Mark Cuban's Cost Plus Drugs has done since 2022, with a June 2025 trade-press report describing employer programs run with partners ([Fierce Healthcare, June 2025](https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/payers/mark-cubans-cost-plus-drugs-partners-virtual-obesity-clinic)), which covers most of what a primary care practice prescribes and almost none of the patented drugs where the money is. There will be a next drug after this one. The process has to outlast any single drug.

## What this does not solve

**Drugs and cancer are national.** A town cannot price a monoclonal antibody. The median cost of bringing a new drug to market has been estimated at about $1.1 billion ([Wouters, McKee, and Luyten, JAMA, 2020, as corrected](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2762311)); public money is underneath nearly all of it, with the National Institutes of Health contributing to 354 of the 356 drugs approved from 2010 to 2019 ([Cleary and others, JAMA Health Forum, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10148199/)). The extraction is national too: the fourteen largest drug companies spent $577 billion on buybacks and dividends against $521 billion on research from 2016 to 2020 ([House Oversight Committee staff report, July 2021](https://westhealth.org/news/house-oversight-committee-criticizes-drugmaker-stock-buybacks-says-medicare-should-have-negotiating-power/)), and the three largest pharmacy benefit managers took more than $7.3 billion above cost on specialty generics from 2017 to 2022 ([FTC, January 14, 2025](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-releases-second-interim-staff-report-prescription-drug-middlemen)). The law has held a lever against this since 1980 and has never pulled it: the Bayh-Dole Act lets the government "march in" on a patent built on federal research when the invention is not reasonably available to the public, a draft framework in December 2023 proposed for the first time that price could count, and no agency has used the power in the Act's history ([King & Spalding](https://www.kslaw.com/insights/articles/the-administration-takes-aim-at-drug-prices-again-this-time-through-a-controversial-framework-for-exercising-march-in-rights)). A town cannot pull that lever. What it can do is keep the record that makes the case: for every expensive drug its members need, publish what public money went into it beside what the maker charges, make that the standing ask to its representatives, that a drug built on public research should come with a public price, and cover its own members from the hardship fund in the meantime.

**Voluntary pools select.** Primary care and purchasing avoid this. Any risk-bearing layer does not, which is why risk comes last.

**It takes years.** The lodges took a generation; the credit unions took 26 years to get a federal law. Nothing here changes the district's 2026-27 premiums or settles the teacher contract. That is why, in the keeper's view, it pairs with the first prong of the [spring action plan](/bigger-picture-action#the-three-prong-plan): a temporary tax bridge under the health-cost exception to the levy cap, which the Board has already used once this year, to carry the district to the 2028 negotiation window described on the [benefits page](/ledger/benefits#looking-to-2028). Temporary has to mean temporary, with an end date and a stated purpose, and the purpose cannot be only to keep the lights on until 2028. A bridge is worth paying for if it buys things that outlast it: the programs and people that are cut now and cost more to rebuild later; buildings fixed once rather than patched every year; a special-education program strong enough that neighboring districts pay tuition to send students in, rather than West Orange paying to send them out, which is revenue that stays; more of what a town visibly gets from its schools, from fields and public events to the sports that keep families here; and the seed work for the record, the primary care membership, and the purchasing coalition above, so that in 2028 there is something structural to negotiate with. A resident with no child in the schools is being asked to pay for a town where the hospital bill, the premium, and the property tax stop rising together, not for the status quo with a later date on it. Taxes buy time; this is what the time is for.

**Legitimacy comes from the record.** The whole thing stands or falls on whether members believe the data is neutral and the decisions are explained. Publish everything, including the refusals. This ledger is the small version of that habit.

## A closing thought

This page was prepared with the help of the same kind of tools the insurers use, as everything on this site is, and the keeper has offered the district the same help in public. The tools are not going away. The companies that build them are moving into medicine itself: Anthropic announced a version of its model for life-sciences research in October 2025 ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-for-life-sciences)), Alphabet's drug-discovery company raised $2.1 billion in May 2026 and expects its first clinical trials by year's end ([Reuters](https://whtc.com/2026/05/12/google-backed-isomorphic-raises-2-1-billion-to-scale-ai-driven-drug-discovery/)), and the first drug whose target and molecule were both chosen by such systems reported its mid-stage trial results in June 2025 ([Nature Medicine](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-03743-2)). The spring [time-economy essays](/appendix-time-economy) argued that everything we buy is someone's time; research that no longer needs the time of many people is a different thing, and whoever owns the machines that do it will hold a kind of power no lodge ever faced.

The mathematicians have said this out loud. In September 2026, twenty-five winners of the field's highest prize signed a declaration that "solving problems is only a tool and proxy for achieving the primary goal of conceptual understanding and insight," and warned that if machines simply announce answers, "the crucial human transmission chain between mathematicians would be lost" ([mathandai.org](https://mathandai.org); [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/25-winners-of-maths-nobel-prize-decry-the-ai-invasion-of-their-discipline/)). One of them described such a result as a helicopter that drops you at the summit: "You miss all the benefits of the journey itself" ([Terence Tao, collected remarks](https://teorth.github.io/tao-web/ai-views.html)). The same choice sits in front of medicine, and in front of any community that uses these tools. One path: members and clinicians each helped by the tools and keeping their own understanding, owned by the people it serves, hard decisions made by neighbors in the open. The other: a system that does the thinking as a single unit, owned at a distance, deciding in 1.2 seconds. The genie is out of the bottle either way. The question is who holds the wishes.

None of that is resolved by a teacher contract, and the contract after this one is closer than the start of this one was. What a community can do in the meantime is boring on purpose: publish the prices, own the primary care, decide the hard cases where everyone can see, and add the next service when the old provider fails. Radical ends, incremental means. The lodges did it for a dollar a year.
